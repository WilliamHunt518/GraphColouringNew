## f-size-decides

**Sentences removed or reworded beyond recognition** (numbers kept):
- Measured the same way on both (the drones the mission needs), a strategy card was chosen on 56% of the smallest missions and 75% of the largest, and a tactical suggestion was requested on 54% and 91%.
- Comparing each person only with themselves, every doubling of mission size multiplied the odds of handing over by about 1.6 on the strategic tier (x 1.62 [1.23, 2.15]) and 2.9 on the tactical tier (x 2.91 [2.03, 4.17], both p < .001), and 22 of 33 people handed over more on their biggest missions than on their smallest on each tier.
- The rule is a sensible one: a strategy card is much quicker than building by hand on three-type missions and no quicker on one-type missions, and a tactical suggestion is not slower than a hand-built plan.
- Figure ? shows the share of decisions handed over, on each tier, by the number of drones the mission needs.
- Both rise; the tactical tier rises more steeply and keeps rising.
- The strategic tier dips at five drones: these are seven particular one-type missions in the two Strategic Heavy games, six of which arrived in bursts, so operators reached them with three or four other missions waiting and often decided before the cards had loaded.
- Participants described the rule in their own words, for example: QUOTE (P29), and QUOTE (P34).
- Dropping every illogical Conservative card leaves it in place (strategic x 1.46 per doubling, p = .006), as does allowing for every operator having played the same fixed missions (strategic x 1.60, tactical x 2.89, both p <= .001).
- With each mission as a single point, the share handed over still rises with its size (rho = .63 on the strategic tier and .69 on the tactical, 41 missions each).
- The two tiers are also linked.
- A mission whose drones came from a strategy card was more likely to get a tactical suggestion than one allocated by hand, at every size (Figure ?): for the same person and mission size, about twice the odds (x 2.10 [1.30, 3.40], p = .003).
- The gap is wide on small missions (70% against 35% at three drones or fewer) and narrow on the largest (93% against 88%), but that narrowing is both rates approaching 100% rather than a reliable change.
- It is a general tendency rather than the habit of a few people: of the 26 who did both often enough to compare, 17 leaned this way and 8 the other, although the three largest gaps make up about half of the total.
- Participants described the same continuity: QUOTE (P10).
- Size also mattered in plain practical terms: QUOTE (P11).
- The size rule also fits the time each route takes (Figure ?).
- On the strategic tier, choosing a strategy card is much quicker than building by hand on three-type missions (27% less time for the same person, p < .001) and no quicker on one-type missions (+22%, p = .09); with the idle time before the first click removed, the card is 44% quicker on three-type missions and level on one-type ones.
- On the tactical tier, hand-built plans and tactical suggestions take about the same planner time (29 against 27 s on average), within a second of each other for the same person and mission size.
- Speed was the most common reason participants gave for using the assistants: QUOTE (P31).
- Where a tactical suggestion does gain is in carrying the plan out (Figure ?).
- Splitting each tactical plan into four phases, the operator's own time is the same either way: a suggestion saves about 3 s in building the plan and gives it back between the plan being ready and being confirmed.
- The only reliable difference is that a suggested plan is carried out about 11 s sooner (p = .006), roughly a tenth of a mission.
- The common feeling that the assistant is faster is therefore only weakly supported: it does not save the operator time, and its plans finish a little sooner.
- Nor does relying on the assistants more make an operator do better: within a person, missions allocated from a strategy card were completed in full no more often than their own hand-built ones (60% against 62%, p = .27).

## f-practice

**Sentences removed or reworded beyond recognition** (numbers kept):
- Decisions sped up within each session, by about 6% per minute on both tiers, and not because the queue grew.
- On the strategic tier most of that gain was gone by the start of the next session, so it reads as a warm-up within a session more than lasting learning; on the tactical tier the second session started about where the first ended.
- The size rule did not sharpen with practice.
- Strategic decisions fell from 11.6 s in the first two minutes of session 1 to 6.9 s at its end, started session 2 nearly as slow again (10.6 s) and fell once more (5.8 s).
- Tactical plans fell from 18.0 to 12.9 s in session 1 and from 15.5 to 8.0 s in session 2.
- Holding the number of waiting missions fixed does not remove the speed-up.
- Participants described finding a rhythm: QUOTE (P10).
- The six participants who said they got better did improve their scores (all six, against 10 of the other 27), but they did not come to use the assistants more, and across everyone a bigger score gain did not go with more use of either assistant (rho = -.07 for strategy cards, -.18 for tactical suggestions).

## f-workload

**Sentences removed or reworded beyond recognition** (numbers kept):
- Comparing each operator with themselves, having more missions waiting did not change how often they chose a strategy card or requested a tactical suggestion, once we account for what each decision was, when it came in the session, which game it was and who made it.
- The data rule out large effects (on the strategic tier, at most about 15 points more strategy card choice going from zero to five missions waiting).
- Nine of 33 participants said they handed over more when overloaded; their own behaviour moved with the queue no more than anyone else's.
- The raw strategic rate dips at three to four missions waiting, but that dip is where the decisions themselves predicted it (dashed lines): deep queues happened almost only in the Strategic Heavy games and late in a session.
- Adjusted for the mission, the minute, the game and the person, every point sits between 59% and 74% on the strategic tier and between 75% and 82% on the tactical tier, with no trend, including beyond the point where the queue list runs out of room.
- This runs against what participants said, for example: QUOTE (P18).
- Nor were missions handed over more often after being left waiting: strategy card choice was 63--68% (adjusted) whether a mission had waited ten seconds or more than two minutes, and tactical suggestions, if anything, became a little less likely the longer a plan had been left open.

## f-flaw-odd

**Sentences removed or reworded beyond recognition** (numbers kept):
- The Conservative strategy card sometimes added a drone type the mission did not use (22% of decisions, nearly all on one-type missions), and all 33 people met it, usually on their first or second decision.
- The 15 who later said they noticed it chose strategy cards less afterwards, and on the flawed one-type missions they learned to skip the card.
- But the dip in their use is not tied to the illogical card itself: it appears early in the first session whether or not they had met that card yet, and in the same stretch the people who never mentioned it chose the cards more.
- Our best reading is that QUOTE marks people who scrutinise the cards, and the dip is that scrutiny at the first busy stretch of the study.
- The evidence is suggestive (15 against 18 people).
- Lined up on each person's first illogical card (Figure ?), noticers went from choosing a strategy card on 76% of decisions before it to 43--52% for their next ten decisions, recovering to 67--68% only from the eleventh on; people who never mentioned it stayed at 67--75% throughout.
- On three-type missions, which never carry the flaw, noticers dipped for a short stretch and recovered (74% before, 43% for the next two decisions, then 59--63% and 75%).
- Many first met the card with puzzlement rather than a verdict: QUOTE (P19); QUOTE (P13).
- Not lined up on the card but placed by the minute of the session (Figure ?), the dip is still there.
- In session 1 noticers started at 86% in the first minute, fell to 38--50% over minutes one to four and came back to 73%, while the others rose over the same minutes (65% to 88%).
- Pooled over everyone the line stays at 61--77%, because the two groups move in opposite directions.
- Several kept using the cards anyway for the time they saved: QUOTE (P28).
- Counted simply by decision number (Figure ?), noticers chose a strategy card on 80% of their first decisions of session 1 and 46--50% of decisions three to five, then 83% at the sixth, while the others rose to 89--94% in the same stretch.
- People who played the Tactical Heavy game first had not yet met an illogical card by decisions three to five (95% of those decisions came before it), and their noticers dipped just as much (-21 points per person, against -32 for those who had met it; p = .79).
- Noticers recovered within session 1 (+24 points per person) and started session 2 lower again, but so did the others, so a weaker carry-over for noticers cannot be shown (difference at the break -6 points [-32, +20]).
- Lining people up instead on the moment they said aloud that a card was wrong gives the same direction (Figure ?).
- Seven people made such a remark; they went from choosing a strategy card on 80% of decisions before it to 49% after (adjusted -21 points, p = .11), and on the one- and two-type missions that carry the flaw from 83% to 35%, although that `before' rests on only six decisions.
- Seven people cannot settle it, but the direction matches.
- A failure of the other assistant had no such effect: after the Tactical Assistant first answered `No workable plan' when asked for a recovery plan (18 people), neither strategy card choice (+2 points, p = .62) nor requests for tactical suggestions (-1 point) changed, and nobody said a refusal made them doubt either assistant.

## f-tier-trust

**Sentences removed or reworded beyond recognition** (numbers kept):
- On the questionnaire after each session, operators trusted the Tactical Assistant more than the Strategic Assistant (5.44 against 4.61 on a 1--7 scale; 26 of 33 people higher for the tactical, p = .001).
- Most of the gap opened in the second session and in the Strategic Heavy games.
- Tactical plans got only a few seconds of checking, whatever their size.
- The gap is a medium-sized effect (mean +0.83 points, d_z = .62), and every one of the six questionnaire items points the same way (Figure ?).
- The usual reason given is that the strategy cards visibly wasted drones while the tactical plans matched what the operator would have done: QUOTE (P32).
- Another put it in terms of blame: QUOTE (P03).
- The gap was larger in Strategic Heavy sessions (+1.13) than in Tactical Heavy ones (+0.52), and small after the first session (+0.37) but large after the second (+1.28); with both in one model, Strategic Heavy adds about 0.6 to the gap and the second session about 0.9 (Figure ?).
- Trust in the Tactical Assistant hardly moved (5.36 to 5.51 across sessions): it is trust in the Strategic Assistant that fell, and most in the scenario where its cards are used most.
- Checking a tactical plan was brief and did not grow with the plan (Figure ?).
- As plans grew, operators requested a suggestion more often (63% of plans with three drones or fewer, 93% with ten or more) and kept the plan unmodified more often (63% to 86%), and once the plan could be deployed they spent a median of 2--4 s on it whatever its size, against about 9 s on a strategy card.
- Almost every suggested plan was deployed as it came: 468 of 486.
- Participants were candid about why: QUOTE (P22).
- The test of this mechanism is fragile, though: it does not survive correction for the round's several tests (p = .065).
- Trust and use line up when people are ranked against each other (the trust gap correlates rho = .47 with the gap in use), but not in size and not over time: a change in a person's trust from one session to the next does not go with a change in their use (rho = .16), and trust after the first session does not predict how their use changes in the second (rho = .07).
